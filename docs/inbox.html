The morning inbox brief 📥

From @techyai_cj. I was opening my inbox and reading everything to find the two things that mattered. Every morning. This is the setup that replaced that — the prompts are the important part, and they work whatever you wire them to.

01 · Decide what “matters” means

do this before any tooling

The reason most inbox automations feel useless is that nobody defines the filter. Be specific about what earns your attention and, more importantly, what doesn't.

Write your version of this:

Sort my email into three buckets. NEEDS ME: someone is waiting on a reply from me, a real deadline, money in or out, or something breaks if I ignore it. GOOD TO KNOW: worth seeing once, no action. IGNORE: newsletters, promos, notifications, receipts, anything automated. For each NEEDS ME item give me one line: who, what they want, and by when. Nothing else. Don't summarise the ignore pile.

02 · Run it once by hand first

before you automate anything

Copy a morning's worth of subject lines and senders into a chat with the prompt above. See what it gives you. Adjust the buckets until the output is genuinely what you'd want to wake up to. Automating a bad filter just means being wrong faster.

03 · Then put it on a schedule

whatever tool you already have

This part depends on your setup, and any of them work: a scheduled task that runs on your machine, a no-code automation tool connected to your mail, or an assistant that can already read your inbox. The prompt is the part that matters — the plumbing is interchangeable.

START SIMPLE: a scheduled run that emails or texts you the output beats a complicated build you never finish. Get one working version, then improve it.

04 · The follow-up nobody sets up

catching what you dropped

The brief tells you what came in. This tells you what you failed to answer, which is usually the more expensive problem.

Run this weekly:

Look at the last seven days. Find anything where someone asked me a direct question or was waiting on me and I never replied. List them oldest first, with who, what they asked, and how long it's been. Be blunt — I'd rather feel bad now than lose the thread.

05 · Keep it to three

the discipline that makes it usable

Cap the brief at three items. A list of fifteen “important” emails is the same problem you started with in a different font. If more than three genuinely need you, the filter is too loose — tighten the NEEDS ME definition.

Start with step one and two only. Most people jump straight to the automation and end up with a scheduled job that produces noise.

The same three-bucket prompt works on Slack messages, texts, and comment inboxes. It's a filter, not an email tool.
